Launching a product is the point where guessing stops. Until real people use it for real tasks, every decision is a prediction. After launch we can find out which predictions were right, and refining is how we act on what we learn.

## Start with what people do, then ask why

Feedback comes in many forms: messages to support, reviews, conversations and the questions people ask when something is unclear. We read it all, but we treat it as a starting point. When someone asks for a change, we try to understand the task they were doing and where it went wrong. The fix they suggest is useful, but the problem behind it matters more.

## Fix the small things quickly

Confusing labels, an extra tap and a slow screen are each minor on their own, but together they decide whether a product feels easy. We keep a steady habit of fixing these small things, because people notice them every day even if they never write in about them.

## Change the big things carefully

Larger changes affect habits people have already built. Before we change how something central works, we make sure the new way is clearly better for most people, and we explain the change in plain words when it arrives.

## Refining means removing, too

A product gets better when something unused is taken away, not only when something is added. We look regularly for parts of our products that no longer earn their place. Taking them out keeps everything else easier to find, which is the whole idea behind [keeping software simple](/blog/why-simple-software-is-harder-to-build).
