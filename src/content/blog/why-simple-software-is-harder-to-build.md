Adding a feature is easy. Someone asks for it, it sounds reasonable, and a few days later there is a new button. Do that enough times and the product does everything and nobody can find anything. Keeping software simple is the harder path, and it is the one we take on purpose.

## Simple is a decision, not a starting point

Most products begin simple because they are new. They stay simple only if someone keeps choosing it. At Upperkai, every addition has to answer one question: does this make the main task easier for most people, or only possible for a few? If it is the second, we usually say no, or we find a way to support it without putting it in front of everyone.

## The default should be the right answer

People should not have to configure a product before it helps them. We spend a lot of our design time on defaults, so that the first thing a person sees is already sensible. Settings exist for the people who need them, not as a substitute for making a decision.

## Fewer screens, clearer words

Each extra screen is a place to get lost. We would rather put the right thing on one screen than spread it across three. The same goes for words: we name things after what people are trying to do, not after how the software works inside.

## Saying no takes evidence

Leaving something out only works if we understand why people asked for it. Usually the request points at a real problem with a simpler fix. Listening closely is what lets us solve the problem without adding the feature, which is why we put so much into [refining products after launch](/blog/how-we-refine-what-we-ship).
